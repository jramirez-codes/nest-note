The dashboard is the app's second surface — where pages go once they're no
longer the thing you're actively writing. These three commands feed it.

## Organize and Ideas

The dashboard shows one of two views at a time, chosen with the pair of bubbles
at the left of the pad header:

- **Organize** — the pile you work through: [Tasks](#reviewing-tasks), the
  [Archived](#archive) pages, and the filing decisions Claude proposes (subject
  merges and notebook reorganizations).
- **Ideas** — the material you grow: [idea cards](#working-on-an-idea) and the
  `build-step` cards a [scheduled build](../server/builds.mdx) files for you to
  check.

**Organize** is what you land on. The toggle sits in the header, so it stays put
while the dashboard scrolls, and it's independent of the notebook badge beside
it — switching views keeps you in the notebook you were in, and switching
notebooks keeps the view you were in.

Each kind in this view gets its own **paged list** — ideas in one, `build-step`
cards in another — built like the [Tasks](#reviewing-tasks) card: four rows at a
time in a card that holds its height, with a pager beneath. The view stays the same
size however many of each pile up.

Ideas is also where an unfamiliar card kind surfaces: it's the generic card list, so
a kind the app has never seen appears there as a paged list of its own rather than
being dropped.

## Talking to the dashboard

The **mic** in the footer strip works on the dashboard, and what you say there
doesn't go into a page — it goes to the cards. Creating a task, moving one,
ticking one off, capturing an idea, throwing one away: all of it out loud, without
finding the row first.

> Add a task to call the plumber back on Friday, high priority — and drop the
> one about the invoice, that's done.

The sequence is one button. Tap the **mic** to start talking, and a card appears
over the bubble footer — laid out as a **dashboard section card**, at the same
width and on the same surface as Tasks or Archived, because that's what it is:
another surface about the same cards. **The words land in it as you speak**, so
you can see the recognizer keeping up and catch a mishearing while you're still
mid-thought.

Tap the mic again to stop, and **that sends it**. Talking is the whole
interaction, so the tap that ends the sentence is the tap that posts it — there's
no separate confirm for a message you've just watched land word by word. While
the reply streams the mic's slot is a **Stop**. Then it's a mic again, and the
next thing you say continues the same conversation, with the reply you're
following up on still on screen while you say it.

For a run of instructions there's a quicker way round than stopping and starting
the mic each time: **while you're talking, the card's top-right corner is a ↑
Send** — where the ✕ sits the rest of the time — and it posts what you've said so
far **without touching the mic**. Say one thing, send it, carry straight on with
the next. It dims when there's nothing to send yet, or while a reply is still
streaming. The ✕ is back as soon as the mic stops.

Said it wrong? The **eraser** pinned to the card's bottom-right corner fixes it
**without stopping the mic**, at either of the sizes a spoken instruction goes
wrong at:

- **Tap it** and the whole message goes, to be said again from the top.
- **Drag it across the words** and it's an eraser in the literal sense. Words
  strike through as it's pulled over them, and are cut out when you let go — so
  one misheard phrase in the middle of an otherwise good sentence costs a swipe
  rather than the whole prompt. A drag that reaches no words changes nothing.

Either way, the sentence you're part-way through goes with it: a half-finished
utterance is still being rewritten by the recogniser, which would put back what
you just took out. What you say next lands cleanly on what's left. The
conversation above is untouched — it's this message being fixed, not the chat
being dismissed. The eraser appears once there's something to work on, and holds
its corner however far you drag the card or scroll the conversation.

For a single misheard character the recording controls are quicker — **Delete**
rubs out characters as it does anywhere else, and **New line** breaks the line
rather than sending, so a message dictated in two sentences can't post itself
halfway through.

The one time stopping the mic *doesn't* send is when a reply is still streaming:
two turns at once would be two agents writing to the same cards. The message
waits in an **editable box** instead — same text, same place, nothing moves — and
the mic's slot becomes an accent **↑ Send** for it as soon as the reply lands.

How much of the dashboard the card covers is yours to set: the **strip across its
top edge is a grab handle**. Drag it **up** for more of the conversation, **down**
to give the room back to the cards underneath. The grip turns the accent colour
and widens while you're dragging, so it's clear you have hold of the edge and not
the page behind it. It stops at just over half the screen, and the height you
leave it at is remembered — including across a **✕**, since that's how you want
this card to sit rather than anything the conversation put there.

It sets a *ceiling*, not a height: a card with one line in it is still one line
tall, and the conversation grows into the room as it arrives.

Claude's reply lands in the same card, as **rendered Markdown** — the same
widgets the editor draws, so bullets, checkboxes and `inline code` read here as
they do in a card. It's asked to keep replies to a sentence or two: the card
floats over the dashboard, and the point is the change it made, which is on the
dashboard behind it. The list re-reads itself as each turn lands, so you watch
the cards move.

It acts on the **notebook the dashboard is showing**, so
[switching notebooks](../start/the-pad.md#switching-notebooks) re-scopes it: in a
subject, cards are read and filed under that subject; on the Sandbox's aggregate
view it works across all of them. Before writing anything it reads the cards that
already exist and matches what you said against them by meaning — spoken
instructions are loose, and without that step a reworded task becomes a second
copy of itself.

Like the [idea page's chat](#the-ideas-page), Claude is told to **act rather than
ask**. It only comes back with a question when it genuinely can't tell which card
you meant and a guess would change the wrong one.

The **✕** in the card's top-right corner (once the mic is off — while it's on
that corner is the ↑ Send above) is the bigger of the two controls, and sits in
the opposite corner to the eraser to match: where the eraser works on the message
you're saying, the ✕ **dismisses the whole conversation** — that message, the
replies, all of it. That's how you get the space
over the dashboard back, and how you start a fresh chat rather than one threaded
through everything already said. A reply still streaming is cancelled. Cards
Claude already created or changed are *cards* — they stay, and the dashboard's own
controls (complete, dismiss, drag-to-delete) are what take those back.

Needs a paired server — it's a conversation with Claude.

## `/ingest`

> File this page into your dashboard — sorts notes into subjects, then clears
> the page.

```text
/ingest
```

Reads the page, files its contents into the appropriate dashboard subjects, and
**clears the page** so the sheet is blank again. This is the "brain dump, then
sort it out" workflow: write freely, then let `/ingest` put things where they
belong.

Needs a paired server — the sorting is a model decision, not a rule.

## `/archive`

> Archive this page — lifts it off the pad into the dashboard's Archived
> section, reopenable from there.

```text
/archive
```

Takes the page out of the swipe order without deleting it. Archived pages live
in the Archived section of the dashboard's [Organize
view](#organize-and-ideas) and can be reopened at any time.

:::note[On-device]
Archiving is a local state change. No server required.
:::

## `/agg-tasks`

> Sweep a subject's whole notebook for action items and file one task card per
> item.

```text
/agg-tasks storage-migration
```

Walks an entire notebook looking for action items and files one task card per
item found.

:::caution[This one is slow on purpose]
Sweeping a whole notebook can legitimately take several minutes. It is the
reason the server's `-run-timeout` defaults to **8 minutes** rather than
something tighter — if you lower that flag, this is the command that will start
failing.
:::

Requires `-root`, like `/talk`, since it works through the orchestrator.

## Working on an idea

Ideas — the cards Claude files with `kind="idea"` rather than `kind="task"` —
fill the **Ideas** view, with a tag-filter bar above them. They read as a **list of
rows, four to a page**, in the same fixed-height card the [Tasks](#reviewing-tasks)
list uses: the arrows and page numbers step through your ideas without the section
ever growing taller. Past seven pages the numbers give way to a **3 / 24** readout
between the arrows. The pager stays put down to a single idea — the card is the same
shape however few you have.

Each row is the idea's title, its due date if it has one, and a tinted chip on the
left. That chip carries the priority in its colour — red for urgent down to grey for
low, the same scale the task pips use — so the row spends its width on the title
rather than on the word *Urgent*. Its glyph says what the card is: a lightbulb for an
idea, sparkles for a build step. (A card a [build](../server/builds.mdx) has taken
over swaps that chip for [its build's state](#what-a-build-is-doing-from-the-row).)
Tapping a tag in the bar narrows the list to the
ideas carrying it (and jumps back to the first page); tapping it again, or **All**,
clears the filter.

A **build step** row takes two lines rather than one, because it answers two
questions. On top, in the row's own colour, the **idea's name** — a step card is
where an idea lives once the project exists, so it's titled by the project you'd
recognise. Under it, fainter and smaller, the **feature being validated**:
*Feature 2 — Sensor ingest*. Named by the feature alone, a list of steps read as a
pile of unrelated chores; named by the idea alone, every step of one build was the
same row repeated.

### What a build is doing, from the row

Any row a [build](../server/builds.mdx) has touched — an idea it was started from,
or a step card it filed — wears that build's state as its **left-hand chip**. Same
badge in both lists, because it answers the same question in both: where has this
build got to?

It replaces the kind glyph rather than sitting beside it. The glyph used to change
shape for these states anyway — a clock for a build waiting on its start time, a
hammer for one under way — so a separate badge next to it spent the row's most
expensive edge saying the same thing twice. One chip, and it says the more useful of
the two things: a card in a build's hands is better described by what the build is
doing than by being, once again, an idea. Its colour is the state's rather than the
priority's, for the same reason.

| Badge | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| **Scheduled** *today at 21:00* | A run is placed at a minute that hasn't come round — an idea handed over for later, or a step whose sign-off named a time for the feature after it. |
| **Planning** / **Building** | A run is in flight right now. Nothing is being asked of you until it lands. |
| **Waiting on you** | A feature is built and the step card is asking for your decision. |
| **Stopped** *feature 2 was rejected* | The build ended before its plan did — with the reason it ended. |
| **Done** | Every feature in the plan is built and validated. |

The two badges that can't say enough in a word carry a second half: a scheduled one
adds **the time it's due**, worded exactly as the picker that set it, and a stopped
one adds **why it stopped**. A long reason is truncated to keep the rows one height
— the whole of it leads the header card on the page the row opens.

Nothing here is fetched per row. Every badge is read off the card itself, which the
server stamps with the build's state each time it changes, so the list is right the
moment it draws and a pull-to-refresh is all it takes to update.

### The idea's page

Tap a row to open that idea as a **full-screen page**, which is where the body and
the full title live: the same editor the pad uses, rendering the card's
`## Problem` / `## Idea` / `## Project plan` / `## Next steps` body, with its tags
in the header.

You can't type into that body — Claude owns it. You change it by **talking to
Claude about it**, in the composer pinned to the bottom of the page:

```text
Split the project plan into a v1 and a v2 — v1 shouldn't need the server.
```

Each turn goes to Claude with the whole card as context, and Claude writes back to
that same card (`upsert_card`, reusing its id, so an idea is refined rather than
duplicated). The page re-reads the card as each turn lands, so the body under the
chat updates in place — that's the loop: talk, watch the plan firm up.

Claude is told to **edit rather than ask permission**. If a turn gives it anything
to act on it writes its best current version of the idea straight into the card,
and if it still needs a decision from you it writes its best guess first and asks
about *that* — you won't get "shall I update the card?". It's biased that way on
purpose: undoing a change it got wrong is one tap (below), while answering a
question it didn't need to ask costs you a whole turn. What it won't do is throw
away sections the conversation never touched.

### Undoing a change Claude made

Because those writes are Claude's rather than yours, they can be taken back. Once
a turn has actually changed the idea, an **Undo AI changes** button appears in the
**top-right corner of the idea's body**, just under the hairline — parked over the
body it reverts, the way the editor's cards carry their own buttons, so it doesn't
push the idea down the page. Tap it and you get a confirmation first — the revert
rewrites the card on the server, so it isn't something a stray tap should do —
and on **Undo** the idea goes back to the way it read before that change: title,
body, tags and priority.

It steps back **one change at a time**. If Claude has rewritten the idea three
times over the conversation, undoing three times walks back all three, and the
dialog says how many changes are still behind you. Turns that only answered a
question don't count — nothing changed, so there's nothing to undo, and the
button doesn't appear for them.

The conversation itself is untouched by an undo, and the button stays hidden
while a reply is streaming (a revert racing a live edit would have the two
writing over each other). The history lasts as long as the app is running: a
change Claude made in an earlier session isn't undoable from here.

### Building the idea

Once the plan reads the way you want it, **Build** in the idea page's header
hands it to the server as a real project it builds one feature at a time — see
[scheduled builds](../server/builds.mdx). It needs `-allow-code` and
`-allow-exec` together.

Two things change on this page while a build is live:

- **The chat locks.** The idea's wording is what the project plan was written
  from, so further edits would diverge from what's actually being built. Stopping
  the build hands it back.
- **The header becomes a toggle.** Tapping it swaps the body between the idea and
  the build's plan, with each feature's state beside it — and the plan opens with
  an **overview** of the problem and the idea, above the features.

Each finished feature files a **`build-step`** card in the **Ideas** view, in its
own paged list under the ideas themselves — same four rows to a page, same pager, so
a long build's steps fill pages rather than a growing wall of cards. Completing the
step waiting on you starts the next feature; dismissing it stops the build, there
and then — the schedule is dropped as the card clears, not half an hour later. It's
an ordinary card — the same complete and dismiss buttons every other card has do
the whole job.

The **first** of those cards is where the idea itself ends up. It takes the idea's
name, body and tags, and the idea card is then deleted from the Ideas section — one
project, one card, rather than an idea slowly going stale beside the build it
already became. See [the idea becomes the
project](../server/builds.mdx#the-idea-becomes-the-project).

Tapping one opens it as a page of its own, which is where a step is actually
reviewed:

- The **composer** at the bottom sends what you type to the agent **in the
  project** — "the header colour is wrong", or "feature 4 should come before 3".
  It makes the change (to the code, or to `PROJECT_PLAN.md`) and pauses at this
  same step again for you to look. See [asking for
  changes](../server/builds.mdx#asking-for-changes-to-what-it-built).
- **Build** in the header says *when* the next feature runs — now, or at a time you
  pick — and signs the step off as part of the same choice. See [saying when the
  next feature runs](../server/builds.mdx#saying-when-the-next-feature-runs).

- **Stop** sits beside Build once the build is running. The idea has moved onto
  these cards, so a step page is where a live build is called off.

Step cards **stay in the list** once they've been answered, whatever became of the
build. They're the record of what got built, and the only thing that takes one off
the dashboard is you dragging it up onto the header. A step the build has moved
past — or one left open when the build stopped — says so in a line at the top of
it, and drops to low priority.

Dismissing the **idea** card stops its build outright, and just as immediately: the
schedule is dropped and anything running is cancelled. The project folder, the code
already built, and the step cards filed along the way all stay where they are. That
applies while the idea still has a row of its own — up to the first step card;
after that, dismissing the **step** waiting on you is what rejects a feature and
stops the build.

Claude's side of the conversation appears in a card in the **page header**,
under the tags — usually a question it needs you to answer before it can go
further, or a one-line note of what it just changed. The full back-and-forth
scrolls inside that card, so the idea itself stays readable underneath, and a
hairline under the header separates it from the idea's body.

Replies are **rendered Markdown**, drawn the same way the editor draws it: list
markers become real bullets, `- [ ]` becomes a checkbox, fenced code sits in its
own recessed block, and links are blue and open on tap.

Claude is asked to lean on that rather than reply in flat prose: **bold** on what
it changed and on the decision it needs from you, and `inline code` — which draws
green and monospaced — on the concrete literals it names, like a section such as
`## Next steps`, a filename, or a flag. A reply is only a few sentences in a small
card, so those marks are what let you find the point at a glance.

With a reply card in the header, that hairline becomes a **drag handle** (it
grows a grip): drag it **down** to give the conversation more room, **up** to
give it back to the idea. The line and its grip turn the accent color while
you're dragging, so it's clear you have hold of the edge and not the page behind
it. The card stops at just over half the screen, and the
height you leave it at is remembered per idea — reopen it later and the card is
the size you set. With no replies, the edge is just a hairline.

The **✕** in the card's top-right corner **deletes the conversation**, after a
confirmation. That's a real reset: the transcript is the context each new turn is
threaded with, so the next question starts Claude fresh on this idea. Anything
Claude already wrote into the card stays — only the chat goes. A reply still
streaming is cancelled.

The composer is the same widget as a `/chat` follow-up box, mic and all:
`Enter` sends, the box grows as you type, and the mic in its corner dictates
into it — stopping the mic sends what you said. See
[talking into a card](./index.mdx#talking-into-a-card). While a reply is
streaming the box waits, and a **Stop** control appears beside the mic.

Closing the page doesn't cancel anything: an answer keeps arriving, and the
thread (plus anything typed and unsent) is still there when you reopen the idea.

Needs a paired server — it's a conversation with Claude.

## Reviewing tasks

The task cards these commands file land in the **Tasks** section of the
[Organize view](#organize-and-ideas) — a fixed-height, internally-scrolling card
with a pager beneath. Tap a task's
checkbox to complete it (it stays in place rather than jumping), or tap its title
to expand the full text.

A task with a due date shows it on the right of the row. Near dates read as
relative labels rather than a calendar date: **Today**, **Tomorrow**, and — for
anything else within the coming week — a countdown like **3 days** (up to
**7 days**). Dates further out show the calendar date (e.g. *Jul 31*), and
overdue tasks show their date in red (**Yesterday** for the day before).

A row of bubbles above the card chooses how you view those tasks — exactly one is
active at a time:

- **Due date** — the one the section opens on. Orders the list by the soonest due
  date first, with priority breaking ties.
- **Priority** — orders the list by urgency (urgent → high → normal → low), with
  the soonest due date breaking ties.
- **Calendar** — swaps the list for a **month grid** of the same size. Days that
  carry open tasks stand out as a filled disc tinted by that day's highest-priority
  task, with a badge counting the tasks due — so a busy or urgent day reads at a
  glance. Use the arrows to move between months, and **tap a day** to see the tasks
  due on it; a back arrow returns to the grid.

All three views act on the active notebook's tasks, so
[switching notebooks](../start/the-pad.md#switching-notebooks) in the header
re-scopes them.
